Consistent Gainers: S&P 500 vs. Nasdaq 100

Andrew Kroeger

This report ranks every S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 company on how consistently its stock price gained over the past five years — not just how much it gained, but how smooth the ride was — using a risk-adjusted score (average weekly return divided by its volatility) computed from 133,557 weekly price observations across 517 tickers. The headline surprise: although 87 companies belong to both indices, only 5 tickers show up on both indices' top-25-most-consistent lists. The two indices reward very different kinds of "steady winner," and the full breakdown — case studies, sector splits, and the tickers that couldn't be fairly judged at all — is below.

1. Being in Both Indices Doesn't Mean Being Consistent in Both

Of the 101 Nasdaq 100 companies, 87 are also S&P 500 members — the two indices overlap heavily at the constituent level. But when each index is ranked independently on risk-adjusted consistency (mean weekly return ÷ volatility of weekly return) and the top 25 most consistent gainers are pulled from each, only 5 tickers land on both lists: MU, STX, NVDA, WDC, and AVGO — all storage or semiconductor names.

In other words, 20 of the S&P 500's most consistent gainers and 20 of the Nasdaq 100's are completely different companies, even though the underlying universes mostly overlap. "Consistent" is a much narrower club than "shared index membership."

2. The S&P 500's Most Consistent Stock Isn't a Tech Giant — It's an HVAC Contractor

Comfort Systems USA (FIX), a mechanical/HVAC contractor, ranks #1 for consistency among all 493 eligible S&P 500 tickers — ahead of every mega-cap tech name — with a risk-adjusted score of 0.231. It also happens to have the single highest 5-year cumulative return in the entire S&P 500 pool: +2,140%, climbing from about $74 to roughly $1,650 a share, with weekly volatility (5.9%) well below the S&P top-25 average (5.5%–6% range for peers).

FIX's business — data-center and industrial HVAC installation — rode the AI infrastructure buildout without carrying a traditional "AI stock's" volatility, which is exactly what a risk-adjusted score is built to reward.

3. Micron Is the Only True Double Champion

Micron Technology (MU) is the single most consistent gainer in the Nasdaq 100 (score 0.174) and also ranks #6 out of 493 in the S&P 500 — making it the strongest performer that is genuinely elite in both rankings at once, not just present in both. Its five-year cumulative return of +1,425% came with 7.7% weekly volatility, calmer than fellow memory/storage names Seagate (STX) and Western Digital (WDC) that round out the cross-index overlap.

That the only two-index "champion" and 4 of the other 5 overlap tickers are memory, storage, or chip names is itself a finding: the AI-driven memory/storage cycle of 2023–2026 was steady enough, for these particular companies, to read as "consistent" rather than merely "volatile and lucky."

4. Nasdaq's Consistent Winners Cluster in Tech; the S&P 500's Are Spread Out

14 of the Nasdaq 100's top 25 most consistent gainers (56%) are classified Information Technology, with the rest scattered across Communication Services, Consumer Discretionary/Staples, Health Care, Utilities, and Industrials. The S&P 500's top 25 is more balanced: 11 Information Technology, but also 6 Industrials, 3 Energy, and 3 Health Care names, plus one each in Utilities and Financials.

A reader hunting for "safe, steady growth" in the S&P 500 has real options outside tech — Comfort Systems (Industrials), McKesson and Cardinal Health (Health Care), Marathon Petroleum and Targa Resources (Energy) all made the cut. In the Nasdaq 100, steady growth has been overwhelmingly a tech-sector story.

5. Huge Gains Don't Guarantee a Spot in the Top 25

Bloom Energy (BE) gained +1,463% over five years — the second-highest cumulative return in the entire S&P 500 pool — yet its weekly volatility (12.3%) is more than double FIX's, dropping it to consistency rank #29 of 493, just outside the top 25. Supermicro (SMCI) tells the same story: +1,058% return, but 12.9% volatility drops it to rank #36.

This is the whole point of scoring on a risk-adjusted basis rather than raw return: a stock that doubles one quarter and gives half of it back the next isn't "consistent," even if the multi-year total looks spectacular. FIX and MU post smaller (though still huge) headline numbers with far less turbulence along the way, which is why they lead their indices instead of BE or SMCI.

6. Nasdaq 100 Stocks Are Structurally Choppier Than S&P 500 Stocks

Averaged across every eligible ticker (not just the top 25), the Nasdaq 100 pool's mean weekly volatility is 5.31%, versus 4.46% for the S&P 500 pool — the Nasdaq 100's broader, tech-and-growth-heavy makeup makes it choppier week to week even before looking at who "wins." That gap persists among the top-25 lists too (5.82% vs. 5.48%), so Nasdaq's most consistent stocks are still, on average, more volatile week-to-week than the S&P 500's most consistent stocks.

7. Consistency Actually Pays: the Top 25 Crushed the Broader Market

The 25 most consistent S&P 500 gainers averaged a +697% cumulative return over five years, versus an average of just +88% across all 493 eligible S&P 500 tickers. The pattern repeats in the Nasdaq 100: the top 25 averaged +420% versus +136% across all 98 eligible tickers.

Ranking on risk-adjusted consistency wasn't just a way to find "boring" stocks — in this five-year window, the steadiest climbers also happened to be dramatically bigger winners than the typical stock in their index, not smaller ones.

8. A Dozen Tickers Couldn't Be Fairly Judged at All

12 of the 517 tickers were excluded from every ranking because they lack enough trading history to compare on equal footing (fewer than 240 of the possible 262 weeks). Most are 2023–2026 corporate spinoffs (GE Vernova, Solventum, Veralto, Kenvue, GE HealthCare) or recent IPOs/relistings (Reddit, Arm Holdings, Qnity Electronics). Electronic Arts (EA) returned essentially no historical data at all — consistent with its 2026 going-private buyout, which triggers delisting from public exchanges.

None of these were arbitrarily dropped for looking bad — they simply haven't existed as tradeable, continuously-listed stocks long enough for a 5-year consistency score to mean anything.

9. The Best and Worst Single Weeks in Five Years

Across all 133,557 weekly observations, the single best week belonged to Insmed (INSM), up +150% the week of May 27, 2024, on strong Phase 3 clinical trial results for its lead drug — and the stock held those gains afterward rather than round-tripping, confirming it was a real repricing, not a data glitch. The worst single week belonged to Fiserv (FISV), down −47% the week of October 27, 2025, after a guidance collapse; that stock also stayed down rather than bouncing back.

Both are useful context for the risk-adjusted scoring used throughout this report: a single week like either of these would overwhelm a plain average, which is exactly why "consistent" is measured relative to a stock's own volatility rather than by its raw return alone.

About This Data

Source. Constituent lists for the S&P 500 (503 tickers) and Nasdaq 100 (101 tickers) were pulled from public GitHub datasets mirroring each index's official membership. Weekly price history (open, high, low, close, volume) for all 517 unique tickers was fetched directly from Yahoo Finance's public historical-chart API for the trailing 5 years (up to 262 weeks per ticker, ending September 2026). Prices are split-adjusted as provided by that API — verified directly against Nvidia's 10-for-1 split in June 2024, which shows no discontinuity in the series.

One row is one ticker in one calendar week. A ticker can carry an index_membership of "S&P 500," "Nasdaq 100," or "Both," so the same physical company contributes to whichever index ranking(s) it actually belongs to.

Rows dropped, and why

  • No price rows are silently dropped from the raw fetch — all 133,557 successfully-returned weekly bars for all 517 tickers are kept in data/sp500_nasdaq100_weekly_prices.csv.
  • 12 tickers are excluded from every consistency ranking (not from the raw data file) for having fewer than 240 of the possible 262 weeks of history — about 91.6% coverage — because a 5-year "consistency" comparison isn't meaningful for a stock that's only existed publicly for a fraction of that window. See Finding 8 for the full list.
  • 14 tickers that are Nasdaq-100-only (not also S&P 500 members) originally carried a finer-grained, inconsistent sector taxonomy from their source list (e.g. "Biotechnology" or "Computer Software" used as if it were a top-level sector). These were corrected to the same 11-category GICS sector standard used everywhere else in the dataset (e.g. Alnylam Pharmaceuticals: "Biotechnology" → "Health Care"), so every sector-based chart and filter compares like with like.

How every number is computed

  • Weekly return = (this week's close − last week's close) ÷ last week's close, for that ticker. The first available week for each ticker has no return (no prior week to compare to).
  • Risk-adjusted consistency score = mean weekly return ÷ standard deviation of weekly return, over whichever period is in view. Higher means steadier gains relative to how much the stock bounces around; it is not annualized or compared against a market benchmark.
  • Cumulative return = (last available close − first available close) ÷ first available close, over the period in view.
  • "Top 25 most consistent" = the 25 highest risk-adjusted scores among eligible tickers (≥240 weeks of history) within that index's membership pool (S&P 500 pool = "S&P 500" + "Both"; Nasdaq 100 pool = "Nasdaq 100" + "Both").
  • All averages reported (e.g. "avg cumulative return," "avg volatility") are simple, equal-weighted means across the tickers in the stated group — no market-cap weighting is applied anywhere in this report or the dashboard.
Data: Yahoo Finance public chart API, weekly bars, 2021–2026 · S&P 500 & Nasdaq 100 constituent lists via public GitHub datasets
